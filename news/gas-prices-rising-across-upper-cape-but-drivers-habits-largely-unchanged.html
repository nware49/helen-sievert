Gas Prices Rising Across Upper Cape, But Drivers' Habits Largely Unchanged

By Lin Lin Hutchinson, Cearra O’Hern, Helen Sievert and Owen Drury Sullivan

May 22, 2026

From January to April, gas prices per gallon in Massachusetts have risen to well over $4. Businesses, towns and consumers have suffered as a result, and with Memorial Day weekend officially here, the Enterprise set out to see how these costs have impacted the towns, gas stations and drivers on the Upper Cape.

Falmouth, Bourne, Mashpee and Sandwich are seeing increases at independent gas stations, but chains such as Mobil, Garrett’s and Stop & Shop are not immune and are experiencing the same hike. Even within the same chain, prices can differ.

Prices vary slightly within each town. Mashpee and Falmouth were found to have the highest gas prices of the four towns, the Enterprise’s survey of local gas stations found, with prices averaging about $4.59 a gallon on the high end. Average prices are slightly lower in Sandwich, around $4.50, and Bourne had the lowest price point at $4.40 a gallon. One station in Falmouth—the Intergas station at Main Street and Nye Road—offered a comparable price of $4.39 per gallon for cash payments and $4.49 for credit card payments as of Monday, May 18, making them some of the lowest prices in town. Nationwide, the average is $4.56.

The Mobil at 548 Falmouth Road (Route 28), which sits at the Mashpee Rotary, had the highest prices of the aforementioned chains as of Monday. “Regular” fuel was marked at $4.59, “Extra” gas cost $5.29 and “Supreme+” gas was priced at $5.59.

A manager at Mobil declined to comment this week, saying all questions about gas prices must be directed to its corporate office. A Garrett’s manager in Mashpee, who asked to be identified only as Bill, had the same response when asked about the reason for price increases. Bill emphasized that he has no hand in price decisions at the 414 Nathan Ellis Highway pumps, and that Garrett’s corporate office is responsible for such calls.

Garrett’s has two pricing levels for fuel—one for Market Pay members, a program that allows patrons to link their checking accounts to save 10 cents on every gallon, and one for nonmembers. As of Monday, nonmember “Regular” gas was $4.57, “Extra” gas was $5.09 and “Supreme+” gas was marked at $5.41. For Market Pay members, each price was 10 cents less.

Bill said business has remained steady despite the heightened prices and that price fluctuations are “automatic” and occur throughout the day, with no consistent frequency to the changes.

Gas prices are based on the market, which fluctuates through trading, supply and demand. The gasoline retail prices are composed mainly of four factors, the US Energy Information Administration website states: the cost of crude oil makes up more than half of the retail price; the other half is almost equally split between taxes, distribution and marketing, and refining costs and profits.

“It’s affecting the whole country,” Tejas Patel, owner of TJ’s gas in Sandwich, said. Gas, supplied by Citgo at his station, costs $4.46 as of May 15. “It’s a bad situation. It affects our profits; they’re declining from $0.05 to $0.03 and so on, as the price increases…I think it’s silly that we’re depending on other countries for gas, when we have so much here.”

For context, the economic recession of 2008 also saw a significant spike in gas prices, which peaked in July at $4.11. Adjusted for inflation, that price would work out to about $6.20 to $6.30 per gallon in today’s market.

Patricia, a Stop & Shop gas station attendant in Mashpee, also said business has remained steady in spite of price increases. She credited patron loyalty for such consistency, as its Fuel Rewards program allows patrons to use Stop & Shop grocery or pharmacy points for fuel discounts. Customers earn a point for every $1 spent, and every 100 points equates to 10 cents off per gallon of gas.

Patricia said pricing fluctuations occur daily and are “all over the place” at 725 Falmouth Road, but believes the station’s prices are steadier than other pumps in Mashpee. The Stop & Shop corporate office determines fuel prices, and Patricia said the Falmouth Road location is trying to remain the cheapest gas in Mashpee, adding that other stations will call to learn its prices.

As of Monday, “Regular” fuel at the Stop & Shop gas station was $4.54, “Plus” was marked $4.94 and “Premium” gas was $5.34.

“[The increases] are just cents,” Patricia said. “But it is adding up.”

Stop & Shop’s rewards program is not unlike the one at Garrett’s. In Falmouth, Garrett’s Family Market operates two gas stations on Palmer Avenue that are less than 1,000 feet apart: one next to the Steamship Authority parking lot and the other near the entrance to Falmouth Hospital. Despite the proximity, the SSA station’s gas prices are typically about 10 cents per gallon lower than those at the hospital location. Amy, the manager at the station near the hospital who has also occasionally managed the location near SSA, explained that because the SSA station is newer, the company’s owner allows it to charge more competitive prices to build its customer base. The station has been open for about two years, and Amy said the price gap between the two locations is beginning to narrow.

Regular gas at the SSA station, as of Monday, was priced at $4.53 per gallon for nonmembers and $4.43 for members. At the hospital location, prices were $4.59 and $4.49, respectively. Amy said the Garrett’s membership discounts at the pump have led to an increase in membership sign-ups.

At Intergas, located just down the road at the corner of North Main Street and Depot Avenue, an employee said the station sets its prices in relation to nearby competitors. The employee added that gas sales generate relatively small profit margins for the station, with most revenue coming from services such as auto repairs and state vehicle inspections. On Monday, regular gas at the North Main Street Intergas station cost $4.53 per gallon. The station is also full-service, meaning attendants pump customers’ gas, a convenience that can contribute to slightly higher prices.

The manager of the Garrett’s SSA location, who declined to be named, said, “All I know is the islanders are grateful when they see us first,” referring to the lower gas prices on the Cape compared with those on the islands.

Gas prices on Martha’s Vineyard and Nantucket are typically higher because of transportation costs. According to AAA, the average gas price in Massachusetts on Monday was $4.49 per gallon, compared with averages of $5.63 in Dukes County and $5.81 in Nantucket County.

“The profit margin gets slimmer as the price goes up,” said Justin Hoxie, the longtime attendant at Canal Fuel & Coolant in Sandwich, a station supplied by Shell, where prices have reached $4.50. “It’s even worse for fuel companies that sell No. 2 oil, for trains and fishing boats. When the prices are so high, my boss has to borrow money to buy fuel, instead of using his own—then you’re paying interest, and there goes your profit. At a certain point, there are better places to invest your money. It’s a precarious thing.”

In the short term, Hoxie said, gas sales have been up at his station, which is situated on Route 6A close to the Sagamore Bridge in Sandwich.

“In a crazy coincidence, because summer people are coming and snowbirds have returned, as gas gets more expensive, I keep selling more gas,” Hoxie said. “It’s mostly due to our location. I’m sure everyplace else, sales would be down.”

Super Petroleum at 246 Main Street in Buzzards Bay had its price for regular set at $4.40 per gallon as of Wednesday. Setting gas prices is almost like a game, Super Petroleum manager Bob said last week, describing the various factors that go into setting a rate, which, like many other stations, involves observing nearby price points.

Bob described how cognizant people are of the prices, noting that when the gas price decreases even slightly—even going “down a penny”—he observes an increase in patrons.

“If you pump it, they will come,” he said.

He also described the “unique” impact of Cape traffic. Located on Main Street in Buzzards Bay, which experiences high rates and volumes of traffic, especially during peak rush hour time, bridge work and the summer season, people tend not to want to exit the traffic flow to refuel.

At the same time, he noted that customers continue to frequent gas stations where they feel comfortable, something customer Richard Jasmine of Bourne reflected, saying he always fuels at Super Petroleum. Jasmine said he is continuing as normal regarding refueling. When asked about the climbing prices, he said, “What are you going to do about it?” He also pointed to the Trump administration for making the situation worse, referencing the geopolitical circumstances that have impacted the gasoline market.

In a similar vein, Cape Cod Filling Station station manager Adam Scena said business has “not really” been affected by the increased gas prices as “everyone needs gas… unless they don’t want to get where they are going.”

Another customer in Bourne, Paula Monteiro, said, “Do we really have a choice?” adding that she had been refueling normally.

The station, located at 156 Main Street in Bourne, also had its regular gas price set at $4.39 per gallon as of May 12.

Climbing gas prices affect the cost of nearly all other consumer goods. Nylon, crayons, Teflon, rubber, epoxy, lip balm, aspirin, sunscreen and Tupperware are just a few of the millions of consumer products that require various forms of crude oil. But beyond these immediate cost impacts of manufacturing or shipping commercial items, gas prices drive consumer habits as well.

Cape Cod depends significantly on tourism for its regional economy. One in five workers on the Cape is tied directly to the tourism industry, and tourism accounts for roughly 47% of Barnstable County’s entire gross domestic product base. But what happens when visitors—who rely primarily on their cars and the two canal bridges to reach their summer destinations and local businesses—choose not to make the drive, in view of mounting costs?

“This is not good, and it cannot go on,” Hoxie said. “It will be a short summer on the Cape. We’ll get one round of tourists come down to visit for the first holiday, and then they won’t come back again. I’ve kept saying, this is gonna be our last big summer for the next 10 years anyway. Once they start doing the bridge work, that’s going to scare people off from coming down.”

As far as municipalities are concerned, vehicles and equipment in the Town of Bourne are not refueled at gas stations; rather, they are pumped at fuel depots located at the Department of Public Works and Integrated Solid Waste Management facilities, Bourne Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem told the Enterprise.

“The town purchases our vehicle fuel–gas and diesel–under an annual contract. New bids for next year will be opened later today, so we are not yet sure what impact there will be on the [Fiscal Year 2027] budget,” McCollem added on May 12. The FY27 budget, which includes the vehicle and equipment fuel budget, was approved at Annual Town Meeting on May 5.

“I do expect that there may be a budget adjustment at the Fall Town Meeting to increase the funding for vehicle fuel,” she said.
