Select Board Agrees On Phased Approach For Watershed Permitting

By Helen Sievert

February 13, 2026

The Bourne Select Board had a preliminary discussion with Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem on February 3 about what approach the town should take regarding starting the process of watershed permit applications for Bourne’s two watershed areas: Phinneys Harbor and Megansett Squeteague Harbor.

Having applied for a Natural Resource Nitrogen Sensitive Areas (NRNSAs) grant from the Massachusetts Department of Environmental Protection (MassDEP) in the fall, the town received $100,000 in late January to cover the process of hiring an engineering firm to provide expert assistance with completing the required technical and scientific documentation included in the permit application process. One of the grant’s obligations is to spend the money to hire a consulting firm by the end of this fiscal year, which ends on June 30.

The goal of the February 3 meeting was to discuss how to approach the permitting process, with the first step being to define the scope of work and expectations for selecting a consulting team. While the MassDEP website offers a basic application, “most of the information will be provided through supporting reports and technical memos that have to be developed by a professional engineer,” McCollem said.

The two harbors are designated as Natural Resource Nitrogen Sensitive Areas by MassDEP. On July 7, 2023, MassDEP changed Title 5 regulations for NRNSAs in towns across Cape Cod to achieve estuarine water quality standards. The 15 municipalities comprising the Cape had until July 7, 2025, to file a notice of intent (NOI), which Bourne had done.

The new regulations had stated that watershed permits in Title 5 are optional, meaning that if a town had not applied for the permit, the “regulations would require that the homeowners in those watersheds upgrade…their existing septic systems to the best available nitrogen-reducing technology,” McCollem said in a phone call with the Enterprise. These upgrades would have had to occur within five years.

The town’s NOI suspended the requirement that homeowners would have had to fulfill. The town now has until 2030 to file its watershed permits with MassDEP and have the department approve them.

“What I recommend is that we issue [a request for quote] and we set it up intentionally to be executed in phases,” McCollem said on February 3, rather than trying to figure out everything for the permit application due in 2030. “I don’t think we could do that accurately and I don’t think we could really determine an accurate cost,” since the final total cost is unknown, a fact that made her “uncomfortable.”

McCollem also recommended that the first phase focus on gaining background information so “we all truly understand what our targets are.” McCollem began outlining her recommendations for what the work in phase one would entail for both the Phinneys Harbor and Megansett Squeteague Harbor watersheds: “Clearly delineating the watershed boundaries and creating the map and coverage area,” conducting “analysis of the current water quality,” compiling “historic water quality analysis data” and updating “the nitrogen load based on changes on watershed occurred since the [Massachusetts Estuary Project] reports,” which are around a decade old.

There are additional “associated tasks” that would be involved, such as determining and concurring with MassDEP the water use range and the start and end data points; coordinating with the Bourne Water District in regard to receiving water usage data for the MassDEP—required annual reporting; determining methodology for annual nitrogen load analysis; anticipating and projecting the nitrogen load over the 20-year watershed permit term, which is likely to last from 2030 to 2050 and monitoring it yearly to see if projections are accurate or need to be adjusted; and estimating nitrogen load reductions or increases that occurred since the issuance of MEP reports.

Communication was another feature in McCollem’s phase one recommendations, as she proposed coordinating a communication plan between the Town of Bourne, consultants, and state and county stakeholders, and creating a framework for public outreach to provide residents with current information.

“One of the difficulties with these watershed permits is that the permit path is not well defined,” she said. “Each town has to create its own permit path independently.”

In an email to the Enterprise, McCollem said it “will be important to stay flexible” in light of this and a monitoring plan will also have to be established eventually, after a conversation with a chosen consulting team, to finalize where the monitoring is happening, what the goals and methodology are, if there are any parameters and determine a quality control process.

“I think this is going to be a very long, time-consuming, labor-intensive process,” McCollem said. “I am trying to think from the beginning how to set things up for the long term for the duration of the entire planning process so we don’t have to go back and redo work or undo work or put different things in place.”

McCollem asked the Select Board to decide to allow her to compose an RFQ incorporating the recommendations, which would be presented to the board again for approval at a future date before sharing it for public responses.

“It sounds like a very thoughtful approach, which is a good one considering that we’re, as you say, kind of testing the waters and doing this as we go,” Select Board clerk Jeanne L. Azarovitz said. “I think what you proposed tonight is a good plan.”

“The phased approach and taking this slow is probably the best way to do it...I do support her way of thinking of how to move forward,” Select Board member Peter J. Meier said of McCollem’s proposal.

Select Board chairwoman Melissa A. Ferretti agreed with Meier and Azarovitz, saying she appreciated the technical complexity of the work. “I appreciate the work that you’ve done to hand it to us in a way that we can swallow it and think about it and be able to take our time so that we’re not rushing and making decisions that we’re not sure of, especially with the amount of money this may cost…There are a lot of unknowns that make me really, really nervous about this watershed permit. So, I agree. I think we should go that route.”

Ferretti asked Department of Public Works Director Matthew Sawicki for his thoughts on the project. “I think the plan’s fine. I think it’s a good plan moving forward,” he said.

Azarovitz, Meier and Ferretti voted to approve having McCollem prepare a RFQ and return to the board to present it. Select Board vice chairwoman Anne-Marie Siroonian and member Mary Jane Mastrangelo were not present at the meeting because they had recused themselves, as both live in the watersheds.

Over the course of this process, “the goal of the watershed permit is to identify which strategies to use…in the watershed based on the data that we have,” McCollem said. While the “mainstream strategies” include Best Available Nitrogen Reducing Technology septic systems, sewer systems with a collection and treatment system, the treatment of collected stormwater runoff, and other options, such as urine diversion and permeable reactive barriers, could potentially be discussed as options. “Throughout the course of the watershed permit...everything will be on the table,” she said on Monday.

“This is about environmental health,” McCollem said. “This is about water quality to support the habitat…in the estuaries that DEP is trying to support.”
