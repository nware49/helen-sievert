Bourne Sewer Commission Discusses Intermunicipal Agreement, Wastewater Treatment Facility

By Helen Sievert

May 29, 2026

The Bourne Board of Sewer Commissioners discussed the intermunicipal agreement with the Town of Wareham regarding wastewater treatment and the status of the Buzzards Bay Wastewater Treatment Facility project at its meeting on Tuesday , May 26 .

The board agreed to review, examine and discuss the intermunicipal agreement (IMA) between the Town of Bourne and the Town of Wareham for the transfer of Bourne’s flow to Wareham’s wastewater treatment facility. The goal would be to reach a consensus among board members on the understanding prior to potentially meeting with Wareham to discuss the agreement and the Cape Cod and Island Water Protection Fund (CCIWPF) subsidy.

As a user of Wareham’s wastewater treatment facility, the Town of Bourne receives a bill annually, part of which includes a capital assessment. Sewer Commission chairwoman Anne-Marie Siroonian explained that the Town of Bourne is “responsible for 17.9% of Wareham’s capital costs relative to the wastewater treatment facility.”

Noting that Wareham is implementing a capital improvement for the facility “to the excess of $360,000,000” and spreading the cost over the course of many years, the first year of the “capital debt that they’re undertaking is $16 million,” Siroonian said. “So we’re responsible for 17.9% of that $16 million, which equates to $2,865,600.” This cost would be amortized over 20 years, resulting in Bourne being responsible for $143,280 annually.

Siroonian noted that through the CCIWPF, the Town of Bourne is “entitled to a 25% subsidy of what we owe in capital for that project. So we get 25% of the $2,865,600, which equates to $716,400 in a subsidy over the course of 20 years. So each year, Bourne will receive a subsidy of $35,820.” This subsidy will be applied to the debt service in the Town of Wareham.

Sawicki, Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem, and representatives from both the Cape Cod Commission and state revolving fund had met to discuss the subsidy. The Town of Wareham was invited, but did not attend the meeting. After the meeting, McCollem sent the Town of Wareham an email on May 6 on the matter; she had not received a response as of Tuesday.

Bourne and CCIWPF share the following understanding of the process: the state revolving fund (SRF) program would “bill Wareham, to say, ‘subtract Bourne’s subsidy from what Bourne would pay so that Wareham would bill us after the subsidy’” is applied, Sewer Commission clerk Mary Jane Mastrangelo said. Whereas it is Wareham’s belief that “it makes more sense for Bourne to apply for the subsidy annually, after making a repayment, in full, to Wareham,” Mastrangelo said.

Mastrangelo said “there has to be some agreement with Wareham and Bourne as to how this is going to work,” referencing the misalignment about who, where, how and when the subsidy is applied.

Sharing the same understanding of the process as Mastrangelo, Sawicki said that, based on the meeting, his interpretation was that CCIWPF would transfer the money to the SRF program, which in turn would send the subsidy to Wareham and subtract it from Bourne’s bill.

“SRF has said, ‘This is the way we want to do it.’ And Wareham so far is saying they don’t want to,” Mastrangelo explained. “So I don’t know how it would be done if Wareham doesn’t accept it.”

Mastrangelo proposed that the sewer commissioners from both towns should meet to discuss the IMA and the subsidy. The board members agreed that it would be a good idea to do so after it has read and discussed the IMA and subsidy amongst themselves.

While providing an update on the status of the Buzzards Bay Wastewater Treatment Facility, Sawicki shared with the Sewer Commissioners that Kubota USA, the manufacturer of the facility’s membrane bioreactor, conducted a trial run of the facility at the beginning of May.

“We did everything but run the effluent pumps,” Sawicki said. A couple of punch-list items—a bad pump and level sensor programming needing to be completed–were identified, which should be remedied in the “next week or two.”

“ It’s taken a long time, but the plant’s in the best shape I’ve seen in a long time, too. It’s really clean inside,” Sawicki said, noting that aside from the punch-list items that will be addressed, the plant is operating correctly, including the facility’s membranes.

“Everything that we can have taken care of ahead of time is being taken care of,” he said.

Sawicki also noted that while at the facility, Kubota provided Weston & Sampson, the engineering firm hired by the town to operate and maintain the plant, information and advice about reseeding the plant. Reseeding a wastewater plant involves introducing active, healthy microorganisms into the treatment system to jump-start it.

Mastrangelo inquired about whether the reseeding process would be documented for future instances, to which Sawicki replied that the town will save Kubota’s written instructions in a manual. He also said that Kubota would provide a report of its visit to the treatment facility.

***

The board unanimously voted to authorize McCollem to accept Weston & Sampson’s bid for the Buzzards Bay Wastewater Treatment Facility contract and maintenance services.

The sole bidder, Weston & Sampson, is also the facility’s current operator.

Sawicki explained that the details of the updated annual contract include an increase in operating costs. The previous contract was for 15 hours a week at a cost of $91,200, whereas the proposed contract stipulates that, for 29 hours a week, Weston & Sampson would be paid $238,500. The new contract also features increased hours during which the firm will provide maintenance.

“I think this is probably the true cost of running the plant,” Sawicki said

Since the board received only one bid for the contract, board members discussed whether to adjust the contract and put it out to bid to see if it could get more bids.

“If we make changes, are we sacrificing something that we need in order to effectively run this operation?” Siroonian asked, with the board ultimately deciding that the services outlined in the contract will best serve the facility’s functions and maintenance.

Sawicki noted that the updated contract “puts us in a different position.” He added that “there’s a lot of accountability that the firm has to have to us.”

“I think this makes sense then,” Mastrangelo said of the increased contract cost. “If we are going from 15 to 29 hours and including preventative maintenance, that makes sense.”

“It’s not that much of an increase, because we are changing what we’re asking for” in comparison to the previous contract,” she added.

“The numbers do make sense,” Siroonian agreed.
