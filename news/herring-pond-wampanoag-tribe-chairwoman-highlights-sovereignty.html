Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe Chairwoman Highlights Sovereignty

By Helen Sievert

April 22, 2026

Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe chairwoman Melissa “Golden Dragonfly” A. Ferretti greeted the audience in Wôpanâak before sharing more about the Tribe’s history, culture and ongoing efforts at a lecture on Sunday.

Now in her fifth two-year term as Tribal chairwoman, Ferretti also serves on the Bourne Select Board as chairwoman and on the Commission on the Status of Women in Massachusetts as a member.

In light of the upcoming celebrations of the 250th anniversary of the Declaration of Independence, the Diversity, Equity, Inclusion and Belonging Committee (DEIB) of the League of Women Voters of Falmouth sought to raise awareness for and discuss the Indigenous communities who lived on the land before European settlers arrived, DEIB chairman Donald Gibbons said. The league had wanted to collaborate with the Wampanoag Nation, he added.

The Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe’s homelands range from the area of Plymouth to Bourne and Sandwich. “That entire region would have been ours, and we would have grown that, fished and hunted, and foraged those lands and survived on it,” Ferretti said of the Tribe’s history, noting the Megansett Trail and Sacrifice Rock.

However, in the 1600s, Plimoth Colony officials allotted three separate parcels of land, totaling approximately 3,000 acres, as the Tribe’s: the Great lot, the Herring River lot and the Meetinghouse lot, on which the Herring Pond Wampanoag Pondville Meetinghouse from 1838 still resides.

“We were under the boots of the Pilgrims. You must remember. And being quiet was a safe place to be,” Ferretti said.

Ferretti said that from 1788 to 1865, local elites were appointed by the state to serve as “Guardians of the Indians,” whose scope was to “take, redistribute and lease or sell Indian lands, using the income to support sick and indigent Indians, and reporting their transactions to the General Court.”

“The so-called guardians, they were not always wonderful to us,” Ferretti said, noting that records at the Massachusetts State Archives show “how uncaring some of our guardians were to our people.”

The three parcels were divided into 111 parcels in 1850 by an act of the Massachusetts Legislature, which further segmented the Tribe’s communal lands into individual ownership of the smaller parcels.

Ferretti said this state legislation was a precursor to the Dawes Act, also known as the General Allotment. Passed by the US Congress in 1887, the act “intended to stimulate the assimilation of Native Americans by ending the Tribal government and control of communal lands.”

Quoting the Indian Land Tenure Foundation, Ferretti said the Dawes Act is considered “perhaps the single most devastating federal policy” on tribes in the United States. The organization had found that “during the allotment of many reservations, the most productive land was identified as ‘surplus to Indian needs’ and sold off to white settlers or business interests.”

Through the land-back movement, the Tribe seeks to restore sovereignty, Indigenous lands, cultural preservation and environmental stewardship, and address the historical injustices perpetuated by colonization.

An example: in 2025 the Tribe initiated the Sacred Earth Land Conservancy with the goal of “preserving and protecting the ancestral lands of the Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe and other land within Massachusetts” to “ensure that these lands remain in the hands of the Tribe and are managed in a way that respects and honors the cultural and historical significance of the area” through restoration, cultural preservation, sustainable practices and community engagement.

Ferretti noted that this “supports our self-determination and our self-governance. When we have an opportunity to make decisions on our own land, it gives us the power to care for it in a way that we see fit.”

Most recently, the Tribe worked with various entities, including the Town of Plymouth and Steven Kahian, a property owner, to preserve and return culturally and historically significant Tribal areas, such as burial grounds, to the Tribe. Specifically, Tribal citizen attorneys Kerina Silva and Jaime Hoctor worked with Kahian to return land at Burial Hill to the Tribe.

This provides an opportunity to foster “healing and reconciliation between the Indigenous people and the non-Indigenous people,” she added, emphasizing the importance of developing positive as well as collaborative relationships.

Since Governor Maura T. Healey’s signing of Executive Order 637 on November 19, 2024, through which the Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe was officially granted recognition by the state and allowed the Tribe to “establish government-to-government relationship with the state,” Ferretti said, the state “[wants] to know who we are. They want to know how they can do a better job at communicating with the Tribe.”

Having been recognized by the state through an executive order because there is no standardized state recognition process for tribes, Ferretti has since been advocating that the commonwealth establish one. Another goal of the Tribe is to be federally recognized in order to establish relations with the federal government.

Federal and state recognition provide a tribe with resources, services and programs for its citizens through grant and funding opportunities. The Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe, whose Tribal Council operates similarly to a municipality, has approximately 200 citizens who elect the Tribe’s leadership.

Ferretti said being recognized affirms the Tribe’s sovereignty. “It is not a new recognition for us,” she said. “Tribal sovereignty refers to the inherent authority of each Indigenous tribe to govern ourselves.”

Tribes are recognized as independent political communities and can create laws, enforce regulations and manage resources, a recognition acknowledged by state and federal governments through laws or treaties, she said.

That includes the 1982 Commonwealth of Massachusetts Aboriginal Rights Proclamation, which affirmed tribes’ inherent right to hunt, fish, forage, and trap using traditional practices.

Rooted in precolonial sovereignty, this is also reflected in usufruct rights “as an inherent Indigenous right to use, occupy and benefit from ancestral lands even when legal title was taken or transferred,” Ferretti said.

Ferretti noted that these rights, through stewardship, support environmental and cultural preservation, as well as sustainability. “It is our relationship that we have to the land that gives us that right,” she added, noting that the Wôpanâak word for land translates to “I am the land, the land is me,” emphasizing that “we are one…we are inseparable from the land as Wampanoag people.”

Noting that the Herring Pond Wampanoag Tribe is a matriarchal society, Ferretti reflected on how her aunt, Verna May Harding, imparted the Tribe’s oral history and food traditions to her.

“We had a herring shed in the backyard. Verna, each year, would go and gather the herring...and she would go out and prepare the herring, put them on the sticks, get her bucket going, send us out in the woods for the herbs and the sweetgrass, and all the things she needed to smoke those herring,” Ferretti recounted. “She never stopped until she could no longer do it. Our garden was larger than our home.”

“We were not rich growing up, but one thing I learned from Verna was that you share what you have, regardless if it is little,” she said.

Ferretti said her family grows, harvests, traps, hunts, fishes and forages for food.

The importance of food sovereignty is significant as the Tribe seeks to reconnect with and maintain traditional practices and knowledge within the community through workshops and events, she said.

“The goal is to teach...these traditions and to appreciate the food that we are given from the Earth and the land,” Ferretti said.

Ferretti also noted the importance of the public learning and asking questions about the Tribe: “How do we learn if we don’t ask? It is always just about educating the public and sharing our story from my perspective and our perspective as Herring Pond.”
