Bourne FinCom Hears FY27 Budget Updates

By Helen Sievert

March 13, 2026

At the Bourne Finance Committee meeting on Monday , March 9 , Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem and Finance Director Erica Flemming shared various updates and amendments to the town’s Fiscal Year 2027 budget.

McCollem shared that the only change at this moment in relation to the capital budget was the Sagamore Beach ramp project. She was informed by Timothy Lydon, the town engineer, that the Sagamore Beach ramp project to upgrade the drainage facility and improve access to the stairs for construction equipment to get to the beach would be postponed until Fall Town Meeting.

He had initially requested $200,000 of Community Preservation Act funds for this upcoming Spring Town Meeting. The project’s aim was to improve coastal resiliency. The area is deemed vulnerable in terms of hazard mitigation.

Finance Committee member Thomas Joyce asked why that was, to which McCollem replied that Lydon “doesn’t have the capacity right now to get it started. So rather than sitting on the money we know we’re not going to be able to touch before Town Meeting.” She stated that “the money would remain in the reserve fund” until it moved forward at Fall Town Meeting.

Flemming then addressed the FY27 supplemental budget adjustments from the initial proposed FY2027 budget.

In relation to town services, she stated that a “$100,000 placeholder for the patrol contract” was added due to the ongoing collective bargaining negotiations.

The town had also received the Fire Department’s county dispatch expenses of $296,798.41, increasing $70,125 from the initial proposed budget. The Barnstable County dispatch, which the Bourne Fire Department uses to handle emergency calls for fire and EMS services, shared the final number with the town.

She also recommended that the snow removal budget be increased to $156,000, a $36,000 increase, which aligns the budget more closely with the five-year average and does not include “what we are spending in Fiscal Year 2026.”

The veteran’s department had experienced a “slight increase in the assessment,” however Flemming “offset that with the expenses in that fund,” making it “net zero.”

In regard to debt services, the town received final numbers for the BANs (bond anticipation notes) sale.

BANs are a “short-term debt instrument used to generate cash for initial project costs and with the expectation that the debt will be replaced later by permanent bonding,” according to the Massachusetts Department of Revenue’s Municipal Finance Glossary. Typically sold by town governments as a way of generating money for large capital projects, BANs are “issued for a term of less than one year” and can be “re-issued for up to five years, provided principal repayment begins after two years.”

Flemming said that the short-term interest cost decreased from the estimated 4.5% coupon rate to 4%.

Excluded debt would be reduced by $225,000, since the estimated borrowing costs for the south side fire station project decreased, resulting in a reduction in the interest payment.

“Remember, that’s raised on the recap, so the offset to that will be excluded debt,” Flemming said. The other debt was reduced by $53,000, which “will be an adjustment to what we’re using out of the capital stabilization fund.”

Flemming stated that, as of now, the assessment for the Upper Cape Cod Regional Technical School (UCT) increased by $128,344 from the initial proposed budget, totaling at $4,438,051.

The Barnstable County Retirement appropriation decreased by $26,814, now totaling $5,221,938.

The cherry sheet assessment increased by $636,910 in Governor Maura T. Healey’s budget, totaling to $6,676,816. Healey’s budget also resulted in the cherry sheets offsets increasing by $48,063, resulting in $934,054.

The total general fund expense adjustment was $534,628 and on the revenue side, state aid increased by $334,096, excluded debt decreased by $225,000 and a decrease of $53,000 to the capital stabilization fund.

“That leaves us with a revenue adjustment of only $56,096,” Flemming said. “So we have a net deficit of $478,532.”

There was also a slight adjustment to the Integrated Solid Waste Management (ISWM) budget. The short-term debt expense and borrowing costs totaling to $298,212 was added, which will be offset by ISWM’s user charges.

McCollem stated the main drivers of the deficit of $478,532 were increases in the town’s cherry sheets, assessment side, including retired teachers’ health insurance, assessment for school choice and charter school reimbursement, and UCT’s assessment.

As discussed at the budget preview in December 2025, McCollem noted that certain assumptions are made when building a budget, one of which is level funding cherry sheets from FY26. “This year, we got a much larger bill on our cherry sheets than we did a check,” McCollem said due to increases in the town’s Unrestricted General Government Aid and Chapter 70, education aid.

Regarding the increases on the assessment side, the retired teachers’ health insurance increased $166,000, or 9.7% from FY26, the school choice assessment increased $306,000, or 24.5%, and the charter school reimbursement increased just under $127,000, or 5.5%.

UCT’s budget had initially increased to $4,688,687 after Forget informed Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem and Finance Director Erica Flemming on February 3 that, upon his review of the preliminary cherry sheets, the proposed budget for UCT was “approximately $380,000 short for FY27.” On March 2, Forget informed McCollem that the budget decreased by $250,636.

Finance Committee chairman James D. Sullivan asked what caused the school choice assessment to increase, and McCollem deferred the question to the superintendent. “I have to put together the total budget using what the school districts gave me,” she said.

“The schools give us their budgets. UCT, Bourne Public Schools gives us their budget and we give them whatever they vote on,” Bongiovanni asked, seeking clarification on the process. “But we, out of the general fund, pay for the school as part of the retirement and school choice. That’s all coming out of the general fund. That’s not affecting the schools’ budget directly. Is that correct?”

McCollem confirmed Bongiovanni’s statement before referring committee members to the Bourne Public School and vocational school tabs in their budget books. “These are the numbers essentially that come from the school committees. This does not reflect the true cost of running the school system,” she said, noting that the numbers noted in the schools’ budget assessments do not include the shared costs that are shared between the municipality and schools. Shared costs include active teacher health insurance and natural gas, electricity and property and liability insurance for the school.

“It’s my understanding that school choice dollars that come in go directly to the school system. The bill that goes out, hits the general fund budget,” McCollem said. Bongiovanni asked if that is by law, which Flemming and McCollem confirmed.

Finance Committee member Arthur Wayne Sampson inquired about locating the “true cost” of running the schools as a separate number.

The cost of running the Bourne Public School district “is close to $41 million,” McCollem said. “We don’t actually see [the true cost] in one place.”

“That’s my point. The reality is that the public doesn’t understand. They see one number and they say, ‘Okay, well, gee, they need more money because they’re only getting this,’” Sampson said. “But in reality, we’re adding $10 million extra in shared costs?” McCollem said that more is added in shared costs.

The end-of-year report that is reported to the Department of Elementary and Secondary Education has the “full cost of operating the school district baked in,” Flemming said. “That’s over $50 million in Fiscal Year 2025.” McCollem added that the aforementioned report includes “all the money that goes to education, including Upper Cape.”

“I’m concerned because we’re looking at very large numbers here,” Sampson said. “We’re also looking at not being able to fund our capital requests this year and we’re looking at a worse situation next year on capital requests because they’re going to come back in again and we don’t have a mechanism to fund those requests.”

McCollem said that if the Select Board balances the budget and has her cut services, she will have to cut jobs.

Sullivan asked when the stabilization fund would be utilized to fill the gap of a deficit.

“This is something that is happening kind of across the state. It’s coming to fruition this year, but I think it’s been well documented that there have been complaints and problems raised with education financing for a long time,” McCollem said. “Ee have a problem here, I’m not making light of it, but it’s not an emergency that would require an infusion from capital stabilization. It’s a structural problem with how education is funded in the state of Massachusetts.”

Referencing her understanding that Bourne Public Schools was anticipating an increase, Bongiovanni inquired about what the School Committee was “going to vote for their budget, that we would have to pay?”

McCollem said that the School Committee has not voted as of now. The numbers referenced in the sources and uses in the budget for Bourne Public Schools were an additional $1,025,590 from the tax levy in comparison to FY26. “I have not heard that they are going to request more than this $1,025,590 number,” she said.

Bongiovanni asked when the Select Board would vote to decide whether or not to have McCollem “close the gap,” what the next steps would be regarding the deficit and if there is a deadline for that decision. McCollem said that she does not know when the board would notify her to do the aforementioned.

“There has to be a balanced motion that is delivered to Town Meeting,” McCollem said. “My hope and my recommendation is that [the budget] is worked out and delivered to residents when the books go to print, which is April 17. So I want to consider that the deadline, but I think the real deadline is March 4.”

Finance Committee clerk Carla Emmons asked if the Town of Bourne has experienced a deficit similar to that of FY27. “I’m sure it has,” McCollem, “Not in my tenure.”

Citing her experience building budgets at companies, Emmons then inquired who would be responsible for making cuts to the budget, as she would “normally go back to the people who submitted the budget,” tell them how much to cut and “it’s up to them to decide how they want to get their budget smaller.”

The School Department’s budget is presented as a bottom-line budget, meaning that if the Select Board notifies the department it has to cut a certain amount of money from the budget, then it would be reflected in the bottom-line budget and then “it’s up to the School Committee and Superintendent to figure it out,” McCollem said.

In contrast, if the board were to tell McCollem to cut, then she would ask the board to provide guidance, have a discussion with department heads again and then present an itemized budget with the cuts, she said. However, she noted that, after having talked to department heads throughout the budget season, “there’s less than $50,000 to cut without starting to cut jobs.”

“I can’t cut things that people need to do their jobs,” McCollem said. “I can’t cut our resources; there are expenses we need. We can’t be effective without tools and materials.” She also noted that she is obligated to notify unions before any job cuts are made, if that were to occur. “Before I could even present the cuts, I would have to talk to the employees, I’d have to talk to the unions,” McCollem added.

Sullivan asked if the School Department and School Committee had a detailed, itemized budget internally, to which McCollem said that while she is not “involved in their internal operations,” the Select Board and School Committee discussed the budget at a joint meeting on Tuesday , March 10 .

The Finance Committee voted to move the public hearing regarding the budget from Monday, March 16, to Monday, March 30, in light of the ongoing budget discussions.
