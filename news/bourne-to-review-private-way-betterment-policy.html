Bourne To Review Private Way Betterment Policy

By Helen Sievert

February 20, 2026

The petition for the betterment of Tara Terrace, Eldridge Street and Eldridge Street Extension continued on February 10 as the Select Board heard from Town Engineer Timothy Lydon, Town Counsel Bryan F. Bertram and the public, who had a variety of opinions.

While these are all private residential roads, meaning homeowners are usually responsible for maintaining them, the Engineering Department received a petition with signatures from more than 50% of the residents of the aforementioned streets, requesting a temporary repair of the private roads. This petition, which demonstrates that the majority of residents support the town performing temporary repairs along the roads, is a legal requirement for the project getting off the ground. The bylaw is written to say that a betterment petition requires 50% of residents whose property fronts the road that is seeking a betterment to sign the petition.

Lydon began discussions on the matter, noting the “snafu” regarding the time of the last meeting at which the betterment was voted to place the article on the May Town Meeting warrant and appeared at the board meeting to reaffirm the aforementioned vote in light of new information.

He also said it was after expanded examination of other aspects of the project that it was learned that two open space parcels on Tara Terrace are currently in tax title. In a memo, Finance Director Erica Flemming informed the Select Board via Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem that this meant that “the town has already secured a lien on the property for nonpayment of taxes. Property taxes have not been paid since 1999.”

Records show the property owner, Prince Street Real Estate LLC, is a “defunct entity,” Flemming wrote, totaling $24,000 in back taxes. Knowing this information, Lydon said, he wanted to review what the parcels’ share of the betterment would be, since “with them not paying their taxes, it might not be likely that they pay this betterment,” which would be $56,097.63. This is approximately 5% of the project, he said.

In regard to a finalized betterment cost, Lydon said the legal requirements for betterment “don’t go into great detail until you really go to Town Meeting and start assessing the estimated order of betterments... It is not only vague, it’s not existent [in] telling us the process on how we get there.” Utilizing past experience and knowledge of the temporary repairs bylaw, Lydon said, he has met with Bertram many times over the past weeks and is “meeting the law in every way from what I’ve understood.”

Lydon recommended tabling the betterment in order to review the town’s road betterment policy due to the recent discoveries.

“We’re flying blind a little bit on this bylaw,” he said.

Select Board acting chairwoman Anne-Marie Siroonian asked if—in accordance with the town’s bylaw—the article makes its way through Town Meeting, then the owner of the open space would be assessed for the betterment. Lydon said she was correct.

Bertram expanded on the significance of the defunct status of Prince Street Real Estate LLC, because due to it being “essentially administratively dissolved” the property title is “in limbo.”

“As your counsel, I did want to say the odds of collecting a betterment on this property are pretty slim in my view right now…I have concerns,” he said. “I’m not saying we can’t, I’m not saying this couldn’t work itself out, but based on present information we have, I just want you to understand that there’s a heck of a lot of uncertainty about whether we get that money back.”

Select Board member Mary Jane Mastrangelo asked about the frontage for the two debated parcels, since they comprise a decent percentage of the project’s cost. Lydon said the average frontage of the 86 houses involved is 125 feet each before sharing that one of the open spaces has 353 feet of frontage and the other 220 feet. Under conservation restriction, the lots are also unbuildable.

Flemming said the assessed values of the properties are $23,900 and $9,800, “much less than the actual betterment cost.”

In response to those discoveries, Select Board member Peter J. Meier asked why these issues were just being learned about now. Siroonian asked what potential options the Select Board had to address the current situation.

McCollem said that while the town has a temporary repair-to-private-ways bylaw, the Select Board has “never set the policy” around the betterment of a private way. “We haven’t had the opportunity to talk about it in your policy-setting role and kind of work these things out as a policy board. We’re trying to straightly apply the bylaw, which has a lot of complications to it, without that piece of developing the policy,” she said.

Mastrangelo said she would be “comfortable with not taking a vote tonight,” since the board has time until Town Meeting to discuss the matter further. “I don’t see how having property in tax title makes any difference,” she said, noting that any property could go into tax title and that there is a process in place to collect betterments and a tax title.

“I’m not changing my recommendation that we go ahead and do this work. I mean we’re talking about a million dollars of work and a small problem because some small properties that are on conservation restrictions and are unbuildable happen to have a lot of frontage,” she said, before also suggesting potential options such as reviewing the betterment policy.

While she did not view the newfound information to be a “big roadblock to getting this work done,” Mastrangelo said the importance of understanding the parcels and potential implications and to inform those who have signed the agreement what the process is.

Select Board chairwoman Melissa A. Ferretti said she agreed with Mastrangelo and asked Bertram for his opinion on Mastrangelo’s suggestion. Bertram said he can review the methodology used to calculate the betterment during the remainder of time before Town Meeting.

“These properties are assessed, because of the conservation restriction, at much less than you would expect for a buildable parcel,” he said. “If we end up putting a pin in it, I wouldn’t suggest that this wither and die on the vine,” suggesting that if the betterment did not make it to Annual Town Meeting, then it could return for Special Town Meeting.

Bertram noted the importance of not exposing the town to significant liability, since betterments are supposed to “improve a limited and specific portion of the community and that cost is supposed to be footed by the people who are benefitted, and not the taxpayers at large…I just want to be careful because whatever we do here, we are sort of setting a precedent.”

Public comment on the topic began with Janet Butler of Tara Terrace, who said that 10 years ago, the last time the betterment was attempted, the property owner of the parcel came to meetings. “Part of the agreement at the time was when the development was completed that this land would be turned over to the town,” Butler said. “She got a letter saying she had a betterment at that point of significantly less money. She said, ‘I don’t want this. I don’t want to deal with it.’”

Butler remained in contact with the woman, who Butler said for the next six months tried to contact the town administrator at the time to “turn this land over.” Butler said she was then told by that town administrator that “because the tax title land had gone beyond a seven-year period, you cannot go after that land and take it… He didn’t know what to do with it. He just never called her back.”

The “parcels have been in default for now almost 20 years and we’re going to keep a public safety issue outstanding because the town didn’t take this land in 2006 when they should have when the taxes were in default,” she said, before citing how potholes have led to people driving on the wrong side of the streets, avoiding certain streets altogether, a girl breaking her arm after getting off the school bus and numerous car repairs.

As petitioners, they followed the outlined bylaws and procedures and committed time and money, Butler said, asking why a tax issue would influence affirming the betterment. “Are you telling me that in every other property that you have approved this betterment on, none of those people were in arrears in their taxes,” she said. “Are you telling me that because the gentleman next door to me can’t afford to pay his taxes for a year or two that a betterment would not go through?”

Butler concluded by asking that if the betterment were not to happen, if the town could assist in some way with making the roads drivable, as residents are “willing to pay to get them fixed.”

Eric Lapre of Tara Terrace said he agreed that due to the unknowns and confusion that the betterment should be tabled, citing the issue with the meeting time for the January 10 meeting and the discrepancies in some people’s understanding of the project’s logistics. In regard to the repairs, he said, “According to what I understand, is that if you own that property in front of your street, you should maintain it. It’s that simple… Nobody’s to blame.” He said he and another neighbor have repaired other roads in the neighborhood themselves.

His recommendation was to “table it, get more information out, know exactly what we’re doing, how much are we paying, what’s being done” to clarify to residents the timeline and finances of the project. Lapre also suggested separating the betterment so that Tara Terrace and Eldridge Street would be held individually responsible and pointed out other roads in the area that need repairs as well.

Nicholas Schulz of Tara Terrace said he came to the Select Board meeting to support the betterment because the disrepair of the roads resulted in one of his daughters breaking her hand and the other being “all skinned up” after falling in a pothole, and having to repair his car often.

He then spoke of what he considered to be the town’s gray areas when it comes to property policies and taxes, citing how he said it was “irresponsible” that for 20 years the two properties were not paying taxes. Schulz said the Select Board “needs to draw out the black and white for not just our community, but all the other ones that own private roads.”

William Chapman of Tara Terrace said the betterment process “was flawed from the very beginning” and that the betterment for Tara Terrace and Eldridge Street should be done separately from each other, noting the deed for his property does not reference maintaining Eldridge Street.

The disrepair of the roads were due to trench failures, he said. “If you know anything about underground utilities, you can see where the gas is. You can see where the electric is. You can see where the water is because the trenches weren’t compacted in the very beginning. That’s where the failures are. They’re essentially inverted speed bumps where the trenches have settled,” Chapman said. “The roads weren’t built correctly in the very beginning.”

He concluded by saying that it would be appreciated if the town would recognize that the Planning Board, at the time of the roads’ construction, made an error, and to fix the roads.

Mastrangelo asked Lydon to clarify how the costs are being divided between the streets. Lydon responded that “it is beneficial to group it as one project and one betterment based on the work being done at the same time,” as it would save money to repair Eldridge Street and Tara Terrace simultaneously.

Having said that, Lydon also noted the discussion about the process of residents petitioning for a betterment and the language of the betterment policy.

He said the law has “limited language on how to assess the order of betterments and everything else is kind of left to the town to create on their own.”

Octavio Amaral of Tara Terrace suggested utilizing part of the project’s 30% contingency to offset the 5% of the total betterment cost that the owner of the debated open space parcels would have to pay.

Andrew Cooney of Tara Terrace, who also was on the advisory committee for the project, said the group followed the guidance and policies outlined by the town and it “brought us further than we’ve ever been able to go in the last 10 years.”

However, citing the vote on January 13, he said that he does not see “how we’re going to be able to move forward with the town not opening themselves up for some liability. My understanding is the vote has already happened. You did vote on this. It’s been approved. It needs to go forward to Town Meeting.” Cooney proposed that between now and Town Meeting that residents and the town work to implement solutions, such as involving the Open Space Committee or Community Preservation Committee

Dustin Thompson of Tara Terrace spoke in support of the project. He said that in general the roads in Bourne are not in good condition. In regard to Tara Terrace and Eldridge he noted that each road has its issue, whether it be frost-heaved cracks near his house, inverted trenches or deep, large potholes, meaning to say that until all the roads in the neighborhood are fixed, the problem will persist.

“I don’t mean to…leave this at the altar,” Lydon said of the Select Board’s impending decision. “I’d be hard pressed if we went forward and you guys weren’t aware of that issue.”

Siroonian clarified that the Select Board had only voted on January 13 that the Tara Terrace and Eldridge Street betterment project could become an article, and that no articles have been moved forward to Town Meeting.

“Do we want to give town counsel time to look into ensuring that the cost of the betterment is only for those individuals who are going to better or gain from this article and that it doesn’t fall on the taxpayers?” Siroonian asked, to which Bertram replied that after reviewing he can share options with the board on the matter. “We’d also welcome abutter feedback,” he said.

Mastrangelo said she would like the status of the debated parcels clarified, such as their zoning, taxes and buildability, the process of taking properties in tax title and a list of all Bourne properties that are in tax titles.

McCollem said she would compile information pertaining to the properties impacted by the proposed article as soon as possible in light of the impending Town Meeting in May, however, the town-wide tax title analysis would be a “different conversation.”

Select Board clerk Jeanne L. Azarovitz made a motion to “have town counsel and staff look at the status of the land and policy regarding private way betterment in time to consider for Annual Town Meeting 2026,” which was seconded and voted unanimously in support.
