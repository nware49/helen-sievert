Title: Bourne Public Schools Presents FY27 Budget
Author: HELEN SIEVERT
Date: 2026-04-03T00:00:00-04:00
URL: https://www.capenews.net/bourne/news/bourne-public-schools-presents-fy27-budget/article_090cfcf4-58f6-4130-a566-9159f8cb9493.html

Bourne Public Schools (BPS) Superintendent Kerri Anne Quinlan-Zhou and Director of Business Services Jordan Geist presented an operating budget of $28,265,695 for Fiscal Year 2027, which is an increase of $547,057 or 1.97% from FY26, to the Bourne Select Board and Finance Committee on Monday , March 30 . As of the 2025-2026 school year, 1,497 students are enrolled at BPS, including 24 out-of-district special education students.

Salaries compose the largest portion of the budget at $16,582,845, or 58.4%, Geist stated. The rest of the budget includes $7,252,253 or 25.6% for student services, $1,816,462 or 6.4% for building operations, $1,542,300 or 5.4% for transportation, $524,202 or 2.3% for technology, $337,092 or 1.1% for textbooks and supplies, and $210,541 or .7% for physical education and athletics.

Quinlan-Zhou stated that budget pressure points include out-of-district (OOD) education costs, enrollment increases in lower grades and personnel and operational costs. “The costs have been spiraling upward at a very alarming rate,” she added regarding the out-of-district education costs. “The need is higher and the costs are higher.”

FY27’s proposed offsets, which are outside of the operating budget, total $5,200,496, Geist said, and include $2,121,130 from circuit breakers, $1,187,900 from military mitigation, $963,046 from school choice, $748,505 from federal and state grants and $179,915 from revolving funds.

Quinlan-Zhou noted that on Monday, BPS was at the State House to advocate for “lowering the threshold and increasing the amount of reimbursement” of circuit breakers. The circuit breaker account, also known as the Commonwealth Special Education Reimbursement Program, “provides financial assistance to public school districts to offset the cost of delivering high-cost special education services to students,” the state’s Department of Elementary and Secondary Education (DESE) states.

Finance Committee vice chairwoman Amanda Bongiovanni asked about the calculations of the military mitigation funds. “It is written into the state budget,” Geist said. “I could not even begin to tell you the nuances of the formula that they use to get to that number.” “It seems to be about the same every year, give or take $30,000,” he added. “Typically, we are coming in at about $830,000.”

“I am tracking where I hope we are going to get and have a carryover and anticipating what we are going to get,” Geist said of the budgeting process, acknowledging that it is “not a comfortable place to be” as he would rather be budgeting “for what I knew we had.” He added that “we have been there for a while, and I was trying to get out of that, and that is clearly not an option” due to the current budget climate.

Finance Committee member Arthur Wayne Sampson asked for the number of BPS students who reside on Joint Base Cape Cod. While there were “significantly less” since the COVID-19 pandemic due to the government decreasing the number of people moving to the base, Geist said, “we are seeing a little bit of an increase.”

The military mitigation became part of the state budget, Select Board member Mary Jane Mastrangelo said, which has allowed state legislators to better track and ensure funding. She also inquired why the school choice is budgeted higher in the school’s budget in comparison to Governor Maura T. Healey’s budget, to which Geist responded that he is conservatively anticipating “school choice at a lower number than that.”

A New England School Development Council best-use report for BPS’s facilities reported an increase in housing trends and an increased enrollment in the lower grades. Based on this report, it was decided that the best use of the existing facilities would be to move the highest grade levels from Bournedale Elementary, Bourne Intermediate and Bourne Middle School to the next higher-level school building starting next school calendar year.

Bournedale Elementary will house pre-Kindergarten through 1st grade and is budgeted for $4,689,907. Bourne Intermediate School will accommodate 2nd through 4th grade and is allocated $4,622,738. Bourne Middle School will hold 5th through 7th grade and is apportioned $5,261,919. Bourne High School will hold 8th grade through 12th grade and is allotted $6,580,171.

Finance Committee chairman James D. Sullivan asked what the 13th grade represents. Quinlan-Zhou explained that the 13th grade is also known as Level Up, a transition service for individualized education program (IEP) students through which they can learn work skills or attend Cape Cod Community College.

Quinlan-Zhou noted that the 8th grade will have its own separate schedule and wing in the school, but will be able to take advantage of the innovation career pathways. She added that the early college program with Massachusetts Maritime Academy is in its second year, with two students currently participating, and that BPS is working to establish a Chapter 74 vocational program at the high school for next year.

Since the 8th grade will be moving to Bourne High School, Finance Committee clerk Carla Emmons asked what steps BPS has taken in light of the cohort of 8th graders leaving Bourne High School for other schools. “We have made those adjustments based on where we think we will be. We put our schedules a little tighter,” Quinlan-Zhou said. “We get down to a skeleton crew of just the right amount of teachers that we need and then we schedule around that.”

She added that “we are using the current staff to be able to educate the 8th grade” for unified arts special classes, such as engineering and theater.

To accommodate current BPS enrollment, Quinlan-Zhou stated that seven additional teaching positions were included in FY27’s budget. Teaching positions had been reduced in previous fiscal years’ budgets; however, the positions are needed again, she said, adding that reductions and reallocations have been made in other aspects of the budget to offset the potential impacts.

An additional teacher will be added to Kindergarten through 3rd grade, respectively, due to the aforementioned increased enrollment in the lower grades. Due to an increased number of students enrolled with IEPs, three additional special education teachers, one for integrated pre-Kindergarten and two for the high school, are required by law to be hired.

Noting that this year’s enrollment of 1,473 students has not increased from last year’s enrollment of 1,519 students, Bongiovanni asked why Quinlan-Zhou was stating that enrollment has increased.

Quinlan-Zhou said that based on an anticipated increase to enrollment and increased enrollment in the lower grades during this school year, additional teaching positions were needed. “We grow over time, every year,” she added, referencing new students moving into the district.

BPS has been “right-sized” for the past three years, Quinlan-Zhou said. However, now with an increased enrollment in the lower grades, those younger students will “start matriculating up” through the school system, she added.

“What we are seeing after the [COVID-19] pandemic is that more needs are emerging at a younger age,” she noted, after Finance Committee member Arthur Wayne Sampson inquired about the number of special education students. “We are seeing younger children needing to go out-of-district to really get the specialized care that they need.”

Select Board member Peter J. Meier and Sampson asked Quinlan-Zhou about school choice costs and logistics. There are approximately 118 students who switched to Bourne schools via School Choice, give or take the day, she said. “Do I think that it costs $24,000 to educate a child in Bourne? No. But when you add those different expenses in from the other tuitions, that is why you get that increased cost,” Quinlan-Zhou stated. “Do I think that the state should give us more in school choice than $5,000? Yes, I do.”

She added that through a blind lottery, BPS can fill open seats, which are usually two to three per grade. Once a student enters Bourne’s School District through School Choice, they can stay until 12th grade, unless they choose to go elsewhere.

Sampson also asked about the total cost of operating the school system. Town Administrator Marlene V. McCollem stated that the total cost to run the school district, not including offsets, is approximately $41 million.

The largest part of that total is the FY27 school operating budget of $28,265,695. The remainder is composed of $8,545,633 of shared costs, which reflects liability insurance, employee benefits, maintenance, and public utilities; $2,458,428 of debt services; and $1,877,692 for retired teachers’ health insurance from the cherry sheets.
